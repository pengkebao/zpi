The Collector of Hours

Every clock in Gregor's shop was wrong, and this was deliberate. He sold time to people who had lost it.

A woman came in on a Tuesday, rain still dripping from her coat. "I need an hour," she said. "From 1998. My mother was alive then, and I rushed through it."

Gregor climbed the ladder to the top shelf, where the oldest jars sat gathering dust. He brought down a glass sphere swirling with amber light. "This one's heavy with afternoons," he warned. "Ninety-eight had too many. Kitchen afternoons, mostly. Radios playing."

"How much?"

"Tell me something you'll never need again."

She thought for a long time. "My father's anger," she said finally. "I've been carrying it forty years and it never once kept me warm."

Gregor took it. The jar in his hand glowed brighter, and somewhere in the shop a clock chimed once, though it was not the hour.

The woman held the sphere to her ear like a seashell. Inside, faintly: a screen door slamming, someone humming, water running in a sink. She closed her eyes. "That's her. That's the exact sink."

"Take it home," Gregor said. "Open it on a Sunday. Hours leak slower on Sundays."

She paid and left, walking lighter, the sphere tucked in her coat like something breakable and alive, which it was.

After she was gone, Gregor shelved her father's anger between regret and old grudges. It ticked there, furious and useless, and he let it. He had learned long ago that even anger keeps time. Even anger, wound up and left alone in the dark, eventually runs down.

He dusted the counter, turned the sign to CLOSED, and wound the one clock in the shop that told the truth. It was almost evening. Outside, the city hurried, spending its hours like they were nothing, like there were more where those came from.
