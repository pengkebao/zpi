The Last Ferry

Marta had missed the last ferry to the mainland exactly once before, seventeen years ago, on the night her daughter was born. Tonight she stood on the dock again, watching the red taillights shrink across the black water, and felt the same strange calm settle over her.

The ferryman, old Tomás, had left a note under a stone: "Engine trouble. Back at dawn. There's coffee in the shack if you're stranded."

She was stranded.

The shack smelled of diesel and salt. Marta found the coffee pot, dented and ancient, and lit the small stove. Outside, the harbor lights went out one by one, the way they always did, until only the lighthouse remained, sweeping its patient beam across the waves.

Her daughter had called that morning from the city, voice thin with exhaustion. "Mamá, I don't know if I can do this. The job, the apartment, any of it."

Marta had wanted to say the right thing. Instead she had said, "Come home for a while," and heard her daughter's silence stretch across a thousand kilometers.

Now, waiting, Marta thought about all the nights she had waited on this island: for boats, for storms to end, for letters, for her husband's fishing boat in the year it came back with three men fewer than it left with. Waiting was the island's native language. Everyone here spoke it fluently.

She poured the coffee. It was terrible, burnt and bitter, and she drank it anyway, warming her hands on the cup.

Her phone buzzed. A photograph: her daughter's tiny apartment, fairy lights strung over the window, a cat she hadn't mentioned asleep on the pillow. The caption: "I made a friend. I think I'm okay. Don't wait up."

Marta laughed aloud, alone in the shack, and the sound surprised her.

She wrote back: "Never learned how. Come home when you're ready. The island keeps."

The lighthouse beam passed the window again and again, steady as a heartbeat. Marta pulled a blanket from Tomás's shelf, sat in the doorway where she could hear the water working against the pilings, and kept the old vigil for no one and everyone.

At dawn, when Tomás's engine coughed to life across the bay, she was still there, watchful, her cup empty, watching the sky turn slowly the color of new beginnings. The ferry would come. Her daughter would come. Everything, in time, comes home.
